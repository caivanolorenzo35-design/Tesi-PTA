\chapter{Gravitational waves} \label{chpt:GW}

    \section{Gravitational waves in linearized general relativity}

    In general relativity, the geometry of spacetime, described by the metric
    $g_{\mu\nu}$, is related to the distribution of matter and energy through
    Einstein's field equations:
    \begin{equation}
        G_{\mu\nu} \equiv R_{\mu\nu} - \frac{1}{2} R\, g_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi\, T_{\mu\nu} \, ,
        \label{eq:einstein}
    \end{equation}
    where $G_{\mu\nu}$ is the Einstein tensor, $R_{\mu\nu} \equiv R^{\lambda}{}_{\mu\lambda\nu}$
    is the Ricci tensor,
    $R = g^{\mu\nu} R_{\mu\nu}$ is the Ricci scalar, and $T_{\mu\nu}$ is the
    stress-energy tensor~\cite{Maggiore2008}.

    In the weak-field limit, the metric can be
    written as a small perturbation of the flat Minkowski spacetime:
    \begin{equation}
        g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\nu} + h_{\mu\nu}\, , \qquad |h_{\mu\nu}| \ll 1\, ,
        \label{eq:metrica_perturbata}
    \end{equation}
    where $\eta_{\mu\nu} = \mathrm{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$ is the Minkowski metric and
    $h_{\mu\nu}$ is a small tensor perturbation of the metric. To first order in
    $h_{\mu\nu}$, indices are raised and lowered with $\eta_{\mu\nu}$.

    We introduce the trace-reversed perturbation
    \begin{equation}
        \bar h_{\mu\nu} = h_{\mu\nu} - \frac{1}{2}\eta_{\mu\nu} h \, ,
        \qquad h = \eta^{\mu\nu}h_{\mu\nu}\, .
        \label{eq:trace_reversed}
    \end{equation}
    Expanding the Einstein tensor to first order in $h_{\mu\nu}$ and using the
    Lorenz gauge $\partial^{\mu}\bar h_{\mu\nu} = 0$, the field equations become
    \begin{equation}
        \Box\, \bar h_{\mu\nu} = -16\pi\, T_{\mu\nu}\, ,
        \label{eq:einstein_linearizzata}
    \end{equation}
    where $\Box = \eta^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu\partial_\nu$ is the flat-space d'Alembert operator.

    Far from the source, Eq.~\eqref{eq:einstein_linearizzata} becomes
    \begin{equation}
        \Box\, \bar h_{\mu\nu} = 0\, ,
        \label{eq:onde_vuoto}
    \end{equation}
    which is a wave equation whose solutions propagate at the speed of light.

    In vacuum, the residual gauge freedom can also be used to impose the
    transverse-traceless (TT) gauge, defined by
\begin{equation}
    h_{0\mu} = 0\, , \qquad h^{i}{}_{i} = 0\, , \qquad \partial^{j} h_{ij} = 0\, .
    \label{eq:gauge_TT}
\end{equation}
In the TT gauge the perturbation is traceless, so that $\bar h_{\mu\nu} = h_{\mu\nu}$.
For a monochromatic plane wave travelling along the $z$-axis, the solution then takes the form
\begin{equation}
    h_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{TT}}(t,z) =
    \begin{pmatrix}
        0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
        0 & h_+ & h_\times & 0 \\
        0 & h_\times & -h_+ & 0 \\
        0 & 0 & 0 & 0
    \end{pmatrix}
    \cos\left[\omega(t - z)\right] .
    \label{eq:onda_piana_TT}
\end{equation}

Here $h_+$ and $h_\times$ are the amplitudes of the two independent
polarizations of the wave: as for electromagnetic waves, only two polarization
states are allowed. Since all the $t$ and $z$ components are zero, the wave
only acts on the plane perpendicular to its direction of propagation. The $+$
polarization stretches distances along the $x$-axis while compressing them along
the $y$-axis (and vice versa half a period later), whereas the $\times$
polarization produces the same deformation rotated by $45^\circ$.

\section{Gravitational waves from binary systems} \label{sec:GW_binarie}

As in electromagnetism, the radiation field of a source can be expanded in
multipoles. We consider a source far from the observer and moving slowly
($v \ll c$). The monopole and dipole terms make no contribution to the radiation, as a
consequence of stress-energy conservation, which implies the conservation of
the total mass-energy and of the total momentum of the system, respectively. The leading contribution to the
radiation is therefore the quadrupole term~\cite{Maggiore2008},
\begin{equation}
    h_{ij}^{\mathrm{TT}} \sim \frac{2G}{c^4}\,\frac{\ddot{Q}_{ij}}{r}\, ,
    \qquad
    Q_{ij} = \int \mathrm{d}^3x\, \rho \left( x_i x_j - \frac{1}{3}\, |\mathbf{x}|^2 \delta_{ij} \right) ,
    \label{eq:quadrupolo}
\end{equation}
where $Q_{ij}$ is the reduced (traceless) quadrupole moment of the mass density
$\rho$ of the source.

The most important sources of gravitational waves are binary systems of compact
objects, whose orbital motion produces a time-varying quadrupole moment.
For a binary on a circular orbit with orbital angular frequency
$\omega_s$, the mass distribution returns to the same configuration after half
an orbit, so that the quadrupole moment oscillates twice per orbital period.
As a consequence, gravitational waves are emitted at twice the orbital
frequency,
\begin{equation}
    \omega_{\mathrm{gw}} = 2\,\omega_s\, , \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad
    f_{\mathrm{gw}} = \frac{\omega_{\mathrm{gw}}}{2\pi} = 2 f_{\mathrm{orb}}\, .
    \label{eq:fgw}
\end{equation}

Using Kepler's law to eliminate the orbital radius, the amplitude of the
emitted wave, averaged over the orientations of the binary, can be written as~\cite{Taylor2021}
\begin{equation}
    h = \sqrt{\frac{32}{5}}\;
    \frac{(G\mathcal{M}_c)^{5/3}\,(\pi f_{\mathrm{gw}})^{2/3}}{c^4\, r}\, ,
    \label{eq:ampiezza_binaria}
\end{equation}
where $r$ is the distance to the source and
$\mathcal{M}_c = (m_1 m_2)^{3/5}/(m_1 + m_2)^{1/5}$ is the chirp mass of the binary, with $m_1$ and $m_2$ the masses of the two
components. At this order, the amplitude depends on the masses only through
the combination $\mathcal{M}_c$.

The total energy of the orbit can be written as a function
of the gravitational-wave frequency,
\begin{equation}
    E_{\mathrm{orb}} = -\frac{1}{2}\,\mathcal{M}_c
    \left(\pi G \mathcal{M}_c f_{\mathrm{gw}}\right)^{2/3}\, ,
    \label{eq:E_orb}
\end{equation}
while the power radiated in gravitational waves, obtained from the quadrupole
formula, is
\begin{equation}
    P = \frac{32}{5}\,\frac{c^5}{G}
    \left(\frac{\pi G \mathcal{M}_c f_{\mathrm{gw}}}{c^3}\right)^{10/3}\, .
    \label{eq:potenza}
\end{equation}
Since the energy carried away by the waves is drawn from the orbit,
$P = -\mathrm{d}E_{\mathrm{orb}}/\mathrm{d}t$, combining these two equations
gives the evolution of the frequency,
\begin{equation}
    \dot f_{\mathrm{gw}} = \frac{96}{5}\,\pi^{8/3}
    \left(\frac{G\mathcal{M}_c}{c^3}\right)^{5/3} f_{\mathrm{gw}}^{11/3}\, .
    \label{eq:chirp}
\end{equation}
We note that binaries emitting at low frequencies evolve very slowly.

Since the characteristic frequency of the emission scales inversely with the
total mass of the system, the binaries emitting at the lowest frequencies are
supermassive black hole binaries (SMBHBs), with masses of
$10^{8}$--$10^{10}\,M_\odot$. Supermassive black holes reside at the centres of most galaxies and cannot be
the product of stellar evolution. It is widely accepted that SMBHBs form as a
natural consequence of galaxy mergers: when two galaxies merge, their central
black holes form a binary system which, once it reaches separations of the
order of milliparsecs, emits gravitational waves in the nanohertz band~\cite{Taylor2021,Sesana2008}.


\section{Stochastic gravitational wave background} \label{sec:GWB}

A large population of compact binaries, such as SMBHBs, emitting at comparable
frequencies and with comparable amplitudes cannot be resolved individually.
The resulting signal is an incoherent superposition of the individual waves,
which forms a stochastic gravitational wave background (GWB).
A GWB is defined by the fact that the
signal $h(t)$ is a random process, which cannot be described by a
deterministic waveform but only statistically, through its ensemble averages.
We will make the following assumptions on the GWB~\cite{Maggiore2008}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{stationary}: its statistical properties do not change in time;
    \item \textbf{Gaussian}: it is fully characterized by its two-point correlator;
    \item \textbf{isotropic}: its statistical properties are the same in every direction;
    \item \textbf{unpolarized}: the two polarizations are uncorrelated and have the same intensity.
\end{itemize}

Under these assumptions, the background is completely characterized by a
single function of frequency, its one-sided power spectral density (PSD)
$S_h(f)$, which describes how the power of the signal is distributed in
frequency. From $S_h(f)$ it is convenient to define the characteristic strain~\cite{Taylor2021}
\begin{equation}
    h_c(f) = \sqrt{f\,S_h(f)}\,,
    \label{eq:hc}
\end{equation}
a dimensionless quantity that can be directly compared with the strain
amplitudes of individual sources discussed in Section~\ref{sec:GW_binarie}.

Assuming that all binaries are on circular orbits and that their evolution is
driven only by the emission of gravitational waves, the energy radiated per
logarithmic frequency interval follows from Eqs.~\eqref{eq:potenza} and
\eqref{eq:chirp}, \mbox{$f_{\mathrm{gw}}\,P/\dot f_{\mathrm{gw}} \propto f_{\mathrm{gw}}^{2/3}$}. Summing over the whole
population, the characteristic strain spectrum of the background follows the
power law~\cite{Phinney2001,Taylor2021}
\begin{equation}
    h_c(f) = A_{\mathrm{GWB}} \left(\frac{f}{f_{\mathrm{yr}}}\right)^{-2/3} ,
    \label{eq:hc_powerlaw}
\end{equation}
where $A_{\mathrm{GWB}}$ is the amplitude at the reference frequency
$f_{\mathrm{yr}} = 1\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$. While the spectral index is fixed by
the physics of the individual binaries, the amplitude depends on the properties
of the SMBHB population, and is of the order of $10^{-15}$~\cite{EPTA2023_GWB,NANOGrav2023,PPTA2023,CPTA2023}.
In Chapter~\ref{chpt:PTA} we will see how this strain spectrum translates into
a red noise process in the timing residuals, common to all pulsars.
