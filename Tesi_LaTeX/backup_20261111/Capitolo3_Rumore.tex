\chapter{The pulsar noise model} \label{chpt:rumore}

The TOAs can be described as the sum of a deterministic contribution, given by
the timing model introduced in Section~\ref{sec:timing}, and a stochastic
contribution due to several noise processes~\cite{Lentati2016,Shaifullah2025}.
In this chapter we describe these noise processes and the way they are modeled.
Although they have different physical origins, they are all modeled as
Gaussian processes.

A first broad distinction is between white noise and red noise. White noise
has a flat power spectral density, that is, the same power at all Fourier
frequencies $f$ of the timing residuals, so it produces no slow trends in the
residuals. Red noise instead has more power at low
frequencies, and it produces slow, time-correlated variations of the
residuals~\cite{Taylor2021}. When two noise processes have a
similar effect on the timing residuals, the data cannot fully separate them,
and part of the power of one process can be attributed to the other. Their
parameters are then degenerate~\cite{Shaifullah2025,Ferranti2025}.

\section{Gaussian processes with power-law spectra} \label{sec:modello_comune}

All the red noise processes considered in this work are described in the same
way. A red noise process in the timing residuals is written as a Fourier series
truncated to $N$ frequencies~\cite{Taylor2021},
\begin{equation}
    \delta t_{\rm red}(t_i) = \sum_{k=1}^{N} \Big[ a_k \sin(2\pi f_k t_i)
    + b_k \cos(2\pi f_k t_i) \Big],
    \qquad f_k = \frac{k}{T},
    \label{eq:fourier}
\end{equation}
where $T$ is the time span of the data. The lowest frequency is therefore
$1/T$, while the number of components $N$ sets the highest one, $N/T$. Only
low frequencies are needed, since red noise has most of its power there.

The coefficients $a_k$ and $b_k$ are independent Gaussian random variables
with zero mean, whose variance is fixed by the PSD of the process,
\begin{equation}
    \langle a_k^2 \rangle = \langle b_k^2 \rangle = \frac{S(f_k)}{T}.
    \label{eq:varianza_coeff}
\end{equation}
For the PSD we use a power law with amplitude $A$ and spectral index $\gamma$,
\begin{equation}
    S(f) = \frac{A^2}{12\pi^2}\left(\frac{f}{\fyr}\right)^{-\gamma}\fyr^{-3},
    \label{eq:PSD_powerlaw}
\end{equation}
which for $\gamma = \gammagwb = 13/3$ gives back the PSD of the GWB in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:PSD_GWB}. Each red noise process is therefore described by only
two parameters, $A$ and $\gamma$.

\section{White noise} \label{sec:wn}

As mentioned above, white noise has a flat power spectral density. In pulsar
timing analysis there are three main sources of white noise~\cite{Taylor2021}, each described by
a parameter defined separately for every observing system, that is, every
combination of receiver and backend.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Radiometer noise (EFAC).} The TOA uncertainty
    $\sigma_{{\rm TOA},i}$ obtained from the template fit may not include all
    sources of error, so it is multiplied by a correction factor, the EFAC.
    \item \textbf{Additional white noise (EQUAD).} White noise not related to
    the radiometer, for example due to instrumental effects, is added in
    quadrature to the TOA uncertainty through the EQUAD.
    \item \textbf{Pulse jitter (ECORR).} Since each TOA is obtained by folding a
    finite number of pulses, the folded profile fluctuates from one observation
    to the next. This noise is fully correlated between TOAs of the same
    observing epoch and uncorrelated between different epochs, and it is
    described by the ECORR.
\end{itemize}
Without ECORR, the variance of the $i$-th TOA is then
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_i^2 = \mathrm{EFAC}^2\,\sigma_{{\rm TOA},i}^2 + \mathrm{EQUAD}^2 .
    \label{eq:sigma_wn}
\end{equation}
In this work the white noise parameters are fixed to the values provided with
the DR2low dataset, and ECORR is included only for the LOFAR
systems~\cite{Iraci2025}.

\section{Intrinsic red noise} \label{sec:rn}

The intrinsic red noise, also called spin noise, results from irregularities in
the rotation of the pulsar~\cite{Shaifullah2025}. Although MSPs have a very stable rotation, they
still show small fluctuations in their rotational phase and period~\cite{Taylor2021}. These
fluctuations produce a red noise process that is achromatic, that is, it does
not depend on the observing radio frequency $\nu$. Its power spectral density is
modeled as a power law,
\begin{equation}
    S_{\rm RN}(f) = \frac{A_{\rm RN}^2}{12\pi^2}
    \left(\frac{f}{\fyr}\right)^{-\gamma_{\rm RN}}\fyr^{-3},
    \label{eq:PSD_RN}
\end{equation}
where $A_{\rm RN}$ is the amplitude at the reference frequency $\fyr$ and
$\gamma_{\rm RN}$ is the spectral index. This PSD has the same functional form
as the one of the GWB in Eq.~\eqref{eq:PSD_GWB}. As discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:timingGW}, in a single pulsar the intrinsic red noise therefore
cannot be distinguished from the GWB.

The inferred parameters of the intrinsic red noise also depend on how the other
noise processes are modeled. For example, NANOGrav and PPTA, which describe the
DM variations with different methods, find slightly different spin noise
parameters for the pulsars observed by both arrays~\cite{Shaifullah2025}.

\section{Chromatic red noise} \label{sec:chrom}

The radio pulses reach the Earth after travelling through the ionized
interstellar medium, which delays them by an amount that depends on the
observing radio frequency $\nu$, with lower frequencies delayed more. Since the
pulsar, the Earth and the ISM move with respect to each other, the electron
content along the line of sight changes with time, and so do these delays~\cite{Shaifullah2025}. The
result is a red noise process in the timing residuals whose amplitude depends
on $\nu$, called chromatic red noise.

Chromatic red noise is modeled with the same Fourier basis used for the
achromatic red noise, scaled by a power of the radio
frequency~\cite{Ferranti2025,Iraci2025},
\begin{equation}
    \delta t_{\rm chrom}(t_i, \nu_i) = \left(\frac{\nu_{\rm ref}}{\nu_i}\right)^{\alpha}
    \sum_{k=1}^{N} \Big[ a_k \sin(2\pi f_k t_i) + b_k \cos(2\pi f_k t_i) \Big],
    \label{eq:chrom}
\end{equation}
where $\nu_i$ is the radio frequency of the $i$-th TOA, $\nu_{\rm ref} =
1400$\,MHz is a reference frequency and $\alpha$ is the chromatic index. The
case $\alpha = 0$ gives back the achromatic red noise, while $\alpha = 2$ and
$\alpha = 4$ describe the dispersion measure variations and the scattering
variations discussed below. Because of the factor $\nu_i^{-\alpha}$, these
processes are much stronger at low radio frequencies.

\subsection{Dispersion measure variations} \label{sec:dm}

As mentioned in Section~\ref{sec:timing}, a radio wave travelling through the
ionized ISM is delayed by an amount that depends on its frequency. For a cold
plasma the dispersive delay is
\begin{equation}
    \Delta t_{\rm DM} = \mathcal{D}\,\frac{\rm DM}{\nu^2},
    \qquad
    {\rm DM} = \int_0^L n_e\,\dd l,
    \label{eq:DM}
\end{equation}
where the dispersion measure DM is the integral of the electron density $n_e$
along the line of sight, usually expressed in ${\rm pc\,cm^{-3}}$, and
$\mathcal{D} = 4.148808\times10^{3}\,{\rm s\,MHz^2\,cm^3\,pc^{-1}}$ is the
dispersion constant~\cite{Shaifullah2025}.

The mean value of the DM and its linear and quadratic trends in time are
included in the timing model~\cite{Taylor2021}. Because of the relative motion of the pulsar,
the Earth and the ISM, however, the line of sight crosses different electron
densities over time, and the remaining DM variations are stochastic. They are
modeled as a chromatic red noise process, Eq.~\eqref{eq:chrom} with
$\alpha = 2$, with a power-law PSD of the same form as the intrinsic red noise,
\begin{equation}
    S_{\rm DM}(f) = \frac{A_{\rm DM}^2}{12\pi^2}
    \left(\frac{f}{\fyr}\right)^{-\gamma_{\rm DM}}\fyr^{-3},
    \label{eq:PSD_DM}
\end{equation}
where $A_{\rm DM}$ is the amplitude of the delay at the reference frequency
$\nu_{\rm ref}$.

\subsection{Scattering variations} \label{sec:sv}

Inhomogeneities in the ISM also scatter the radio waves, so that part of the
signal reaches the observer along longer paths. This multipath propagation
broadens the pulse with an exponential tail, whose characteristic timescale
$\tau_{\rm sc}$ depends strongly on the radio
frequency~\cite{Shaifullah2025},
\begin{equation}
    \tau_{\rm sc} \propto \nu^{-\alpha}.
    \label{eq:scattering}
\end{equation}
Since the line of sight changes with time, the scattering also varies and
introduces an additional chromatic red noise in the TOAs. It is modeled with
Eq.~\eqref{eq:chrom} and a power-law PSD of the same form as
Eq.~\eqref{eq:PSD_DM}, with amplitude $A_{\rm SV}$ and spectral index
$\gamma_{\rm SV}$.

The value of $\alpha$ depends on the properties of the scattering medium. A
thin screen with inhomogeneities of the same size gives $\alpha = 4$, which is
the value adopted for the DR2low dataset, while an extended medium with
Kolmogorov turbulence gives $\alpha = 4.4$~\cite{Iraci2025}. In this work we use $\alpha = 4$ as
the reference model, and we study the effect of $\alpha = 4.4$ and of a free
chromatic index in Chapter~\ref{chpt:degenerazioni}.

\subsection{Other chromatic noise processes} \label{sec:altri_chrom}

DM variations and scattering are not the only chromatic effects in pulsar
timing. The solar wind adds a further contribution to the DM, which is largest
when the line of sight passes close to the Sun and therefore varies along the
year. Other effects include sudden chromatic events, such as exponential dips
in the residuals, and additional delays caused by refraction in the
ISM~\cite{Shaifullah2025,Iraci2025}. For
PSR~J1640+2224, which lies far from the ecliptic plane, the solar wind is
described only by a deterministic term in the timing model~\cite{Iraci2025}, and none of these
processes is included in the noise model. The only additional noise process
considered in this work is the band noise, discussed in Section~\ref{sec:bn}.


\section{Band noise} \label{sec:bn}

Band noise is a red noise process that is present only in the TOAs observed
within a given radio frequency band~\cite{Lentati2016}. In this work the band considered is the
LOFAR band, 120--190\,MHz, which contains the TOAs recorded by the seven LOFAR
observing systems of the DR2low dataset.

The physical origin of band noise is not known with certainty. It may come from
effects of the ISM that are not coherent between different bands, for example
because photons at different frequencies cross different scattering volumes, or
that scale with frequency more steeply than $\nu^{-2}$, and therefore appear as
an excess of noise at low frequencies. Alternatively, it may be caused by
frequency-dependent calibration errors, which can remain correlated over
hundreds of days, or by radio frequency interference (RFI) present in the same
band at different sites. RFI, however, is expected to be mostly uncorrelated
between observations, and in that case it is absorbed by the white noise
parameters. In the first IPTA data release, band noise was detected in four
pulsars, with amplitudes $\log_{10}A_{\rm BN}$ between about $-14$ and $-12$
and spectral indices $\gamma_{\rm BN}$ between about $0.6$ and
$3.3$~\cite{Lentati2016}.

Band noise must be distinguished from system noise, a red noise process that
affects only the TOAs of a single observing system, that is, a single
combination of telescope, receiver and backend, and that can arise from
instrumental effects or calibration errors. If a band contains only one
system, the two models act on exactly the same TOAs and are completely
covariant, so that the data cannot distinguish between them. When several
systems observe in the same band, as for the seven LOFAR systems used here, a
noise process common to all of them can instead be attributed to the band. For
this reason, low-frequency observations such as those of LOFAR, together with
wide-band receivers, are particularly useful to identify band-dependent
effects~\cite{Lentati2016}.

The band noise is modeled as a power law of the same form as
Eq.~\eqref{eq:PSD_RN}, with amplitude $A_{\rm BN}$ and spectral index
$\gamma_{\rm BN}$. The Fourier basis of Section~\ref{sec:modello_comune} is
applied only to the TOAs of the LOFAR band and is set to zero for all the other
TOAs. Unlike the chromatic noise of Eq.~\eqref{eq:chrom}, no factor
$(\nu_{\rm ref}/\nu_i)^{\alpha}$ is included, since the dependence on the radio
frequency enters only through the selection of the TOAs. We use $N = 50$
Fourier components. This number was fixed a priori and was not obtained from a
model selection.

\section{Noise model for PSR~J1640+2224}\label{NM}

In summary, the reference noise model used in this work for PSR~J1640+2224
contains the white noise, the intrinsic red noise, the DM variations and the
scattering variations with $\alpha = 4$. The white noise parameters are fixed,
with an EFAC and an EQUAD for each of the 13 observing systems and an ECORR for
each of the seven LOFAR systems, so that the free parameters are the six
amplitudes and spectral indices of the three red noise processes. In
Chapter~\ref{chpt:degenerazioni} we also consider three variants of this
model, one with $\alpha = 4.4$, one in which $\alpha$ is a free parameter, and
one that includes the band noise of Section~\ref{sec:bn}. The number of
Fourier components and the priors of each process are given in
Section~\ref{sec:bayes}.
