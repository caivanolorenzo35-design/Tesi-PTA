\chapter{Bayesian inference and analysis tools} \label{chpt:inferenza}

The noise model described in Chapter~\ref{chpt:rumore} depends on a small
number of parameters, the amplitudes and spectral indices of the noise
processes. In this chapter we describe how these parameters are estimated from
the timing residuals. We first introduce Bayesian inference, then the
likelihood used in pulsar timing analysis, and finally the method used to
sample the posterior distribution.

\section{Bayesian inference} \label{sec:bayes}

In Bayesian inference, the result of a measurement is the posterior
distribution $p(\bm{\theta}|\delta\bm{t})$, that is, the probability density
of the model parameters $\bm{\theta}$ given the data, which in our case are the
timing residuals $\delta\bm{t}$. The posterior is given by Bayes' theorem,
\begin{equation}
    p(\bm{\theta}|\delta\bm{t}) =
    \frac{\mathcal{L}(\delta\bm{t}|\bm{\theta})\,\pi(\bm{\theta})}{\mathcal{Z}},
    \label{eq:bayes}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}(\delta\bm{t}|\bm{\theta})$ is the likelihood, the
probability density of the data given the parameters, and $\pi(\bm{\theta})$ is
the prior, which describes what we know about the parameters before the
measurement. The denominator is the evidence,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{Z} = \int \mathcal{L}(\delta\bm{t}|\bm{\theta})\,
    \pi(\bm{\theta})\,\dd\bm{\theta},
    \label{eq:evidence}
\end{equation}
which does not depend on $\bm{\theta}$. For parameter estimation it is only a
normalization constant, while it becomes important when different models are
compared. % , as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:model_selection}.  [rimando alla 4.4, commentata]

In the reference model of this work the free parameters are the amplitudes and
spectral indices of the intrinsic red noise, of the DM variations and of the
scattering variations, modeled with $54$, $40$ and $40$ Fourier components
respectively. For all the amplitudes we use a prior uniform in $\log_{10}A$
between $-20$ and $-11$, and for all the spectral indices a uniform prior
between $0$ and $7$. A prior uniform in the logarithm is the usual choice for
a quantity whose order of magnitude is not known. The same priors are used for
the band noise, and when the chromatic index $\alpha$ is left free its prior is
uniform between $0$ and $10$.

The posterior is a function of all the parameters. To study a single parameter
$\theta_i$ we integrate the posterior over all the others, obtaining the
marginalized posterior
\begin{equation}
    p(\theta_i|\delta\bm{t}) = \int p(\bm{\theta}|\delta\bm{t})
    \prod_{k\neq i}\dd\theta_k .
    \label{eq:marginal}
\end{equation}
In this way the uncertainty on the other parameters is included in the result
for $\theta_i$. In particular, if two parameters are covariant, marginalizing
over one of them broadens the posterior of the other.

\section{The pulsar timing likelihood} \label{sec:likelihood}

To write the likelihood we need a model for the timing residuals. We write
them as the sum of three contributions,
\begin{equation}
    \delta\bm{t} = \bm{M}\bm{\epsilon} + \bm{F}\bm{a} + \bm{n}_{\rm WN}.
    \label{eq:modello_residui}
\end{equation}
The first term accounts for the timing model. The timing model parameters are
obtained from a fit performed before the noise analysis, and this fit may have
absorbed part of the noise. Since the corrections are small, the timing model
is linearized around the best-fit values. The design matrix $\bm{M}$ contains
the derivatives of the TOAs with respect to the timing model parameters, and
$\bm{\epsilon}$ is the vector of the small offsets of these parameters from
their best-fit values. The second term contains the red noise processes of
Chapter~\ref{chpt:rumore}. The columns of $\bm{F}$ are the sines and cosines of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:fourier}, multiplied by the chromatic factor of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:chrom} for the chromatic processes, and $\bm{a}$ is the vector of
the Fourier coefficients $a_k$ and $b_k$ of all the processes. The last term,
$\bm{n}_{\rm WN}$, is the white noise. It is Gaussian with covariance matrix
$\bm{C}_{\rm WN}$, whose diagonal elements are the variances $\sigma_i^2$ of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:sigma_wn}, while ECORR adds a correlation between the TOAs of
the same LOFAR system observed at the same epoch.

For fixed $\bm{\epsilon}$ and $\bm{a}$, the quantity
$\delta\bm{t} - \bm{M}\bm{\epsilon} - \bm{F}\bm{a}$ is only white noise, so the
likelihood is a Gaussian. However, we are not interested in the values of
$\bm{\epsilon}$ and $\bm{a}$, which describe one particular realization of the
noise, but in the parameters $\bm{\theta}$ of the power spectral densities.
The coefficients $\bm{a}$ have a Gaussian prior with the variances of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:varianza_coeff}, which depend on $\bm{\theta}$ through the PSD,
while for $\bm{\epsilon}$ we use a uniform prior. With these choices the
integral over $\bm{\epsilon}$ and $\bm{a}$ can be computed analytically, and
the result is again a Gaussian,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}(\delta\bm{t}|\bm{\theta}) =
    \frac{\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\,\delta\bm{t}^{T}\bm{C}^{-1}\delta\bm{t}\right)}
    {\sqrt{\det(2\pi\bm{C})}},
    \label{eq:likelihood}
\end{equation}
with covariance matrix
\begin{equation}
    \bm{C}(\bm{\theta}) = \bm{C}_{\rm WN} + \bm{F}\,\bm{\Phi}(\bm{\theta})\,\bm{F}^{T},
    \label{eq:covarianza}
\end{equation}
where $\bm{\Phi}$ is the diagonal matrix of the variances of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:varianza_coeff}. The marginalization over $\bm{\epsilon}$ adds to
$\bm{C}$ a term of the same form for the timing model, with $\bm{M}$ in place
of $\bm{F}$ and a very large variance, which corresponds to the uniform prior.

This marginalized likelihood depends only on $\bm{\theta}$. Since the white
noise parameters are fixed, in the reference model it is a function of six
parameters.

\section{Sampling the posterior} \label{sec:mcmc}

The posterior of Eq.~\eqref{eq:bayes} cannot be computed analytically, and
evaluating it on a grid becomes too expensive as the number of parameters
grows. We therefore use a Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method, which
produces a chain of samples of $\bm{\theta}$ distributed according to the
posterior. The marginalized posterior of a parameter is then simply the
histogram of its samples.

The chain is built with the Metropolis--Hastings algorithm. It starts from a
point drawn from the prior. At each iteration a new point is proposed, and the
posterior at the proposed point is compared with the posterior at the current
one. If it is larger the move is accepted, otherwise it is accepted with
probability equal to the ratio of the two values. If the move is rejected, the
chain stays at the current point. Since only ratios of the posterior are
needed, the evidence does not have to be computed. We use the
\textsc{PTMCMCSampler} package, which combines several proposal distributions
that are adapted during the run using the previous samples of the chain.

Each chain consists of $10^6$ iterations, of which one sample every $10$ is
saved. The first part of the chain depends on the starting point and does not
represent the posterior, so we discard the first $25\%$ of the samples as
burn-in.

Consecutive samples of the chain are correlated, so the number of independent
samples is smaller than the length of the chain. The correlation is measured
by the integrated autocorrelation time $\tau_{\rm ac}$, expressed in units of
saved samples, which we estimate for each parameter. The effective number of
samples is then
\begin{equation}
    N_{\rm eff} = \frac{N_{\rm s}}{\max_i \tau_{{\rm ac},i}},
    \label{eq:neff}
\end{equation}
where $N_{\rm s}$ is the number of samples left after the burn-in and the
maximum is taken over the parameters. We use $N_{\rm eff}$ to check that the
chain is long enough.

The results are shown as corner plots. The panels on the diagonal show the
marginalized posterior of each parameter, and the other panels show the
two-dimensional marginalized posterior of each pair of parameters, with
contours enclosing $68\%$, $95\%$ and $99.7\%$ of the probability. The
two-dimensional panels show the covariance between the parameters, and are
therefore the main tool to identify degeneracies.

\section{Enterprise and Discovery} \label{sec:software}

The likelihood of Eq.~\eqref{eq:likelihood} is implemented in
\textsc{Enterprise}, the software commonly used in PTA analyses, which was
also used for the noise analysis of the DR2low dataset. It is written in
Python and relies on the NumPy and SciPy libraries for its linear algebra
operations. \textsc{Discovery} is a more recent package that implements the
same likelihood with the JAX library.

JAX is a Python library for numerical computing. A function written with JAX
can be compiled just in time, that is, it is translated into optimized code the
first time it is called, so that the following calls are much faster. JAX
functions can also be differentiated automatically and executed on graphics
processing units (GPUs). In this work we use only the first feature. The
likelihood is compiled and evaluated on CPUs in double precision, and its
gradient is not needed by the Metropolis--Hastings algorithm.

In Chapter~\ref{chpt:risultati} we compare the posteriors obtained with the
two codes for the same model and the same priors. Since both analyses use
\textsc{PTMCMCSampler}, the comparison is a test of the implementation of the
likelihood. The autocorrelation times are computed with the \textsc{emcee}
package and the corner plots are made with the \textsc{corner} package.
