\chapter{Pulsar Timing Array} \label{chpt:PTA}

Pulsar timing arrays (PTAs)~\cite{Sazhin1978, Detweiler1979, FosterBacker1990} are currently the most sensitive method for detecting
gravitational waves in the nanohertz band, where the signal is expected to be
dominated by the population of supermassive black hole binaries (SMBHBs) discussed
in Section~\ref{sec:GW_binarie}.

\section{Pulsar timing} \label{sec:timing}

The PTA technique relies on pulsars, which are rapidly
rotating and highly magnetized neutron stars~\cite{Hewish1968} that emit beams of radio radiation along
their magnetic axis. Pulsars are commonly classified according to their rotational
period into classical pulsars, with periods of the order of one second, and
millisecond pulsars (MSPs)~\cite{Backer1982}, with periods of a few milliseconds. MSPs have a much more stable rotation, which allows the
times of arrival (TOAs) of their pulses to be measured with higher precision.

The measurement of the TOAs involves several steps~\cite{LorimerKramer2005, Taylor2021}. The radio emission of the
pulsar propagates through the ionized interstellar medium (ISM), whose refractive
index depends on frequency. As a consequence, the signal suffers a
frequency-dependent delay proportional to $\nu^{-2}$, where $\nu$ is the observing
radio frequency, so that higher radio frequencies arrive earlier than
lower ones.
This dispersion can be corrected in two ways. In incoherent dedispersion, the
observed band is divided into narrow frequency channels, and each channel is
shifted in time according to the $\nu^{-2}$ law before summing them, so that the
pulse is aligned across the whole band. In coherent dedispersion, the raw signal
is instead convolved with the inverse transfer function of the ISM, which removes
the dispersive delay exactly.

Individual pulses are usually too weak to be distinguished from the background
noise. To overcome this problem, the data are divided into segments of length
equal to the pulsar period and summed together, a procedure known as folding.
Since the pulsed signal always falls at the same rotational phase, it adds up
coherently, while the noise averages out, so that the signal-to-noise ratio grows
as the square root of the number of folded pulses. The result is an average pulse
profile which, unlike individual pulses, is remarkably stable over time, and which
is used to determine the TOA.

The TOA is then obtained by cross-correlating the folded profile with a
high signal-to-noise template of the same pulsar. The resulting phase shift,
converted into time and added to the reference time of the observation, gives
the arrival time of the pulse together with its uncertainty.

Since the TOAs are measured at the observatory, which is not an inertial
reference frame, they are then referred to the Solar System barycenter (SSB)
through a series of clock and propagation corrections. The barycentric TOAs are
finally compared with the predictions of a timing model, which describes the
rotation of the pulsar together with its astrometric and, for binary systems,
orbital parameters. In this work we use the timing solution of the EPTA DR2low
dataset~\cite{Iraci2025}, which combines the EPTA second data
release~\cite{EPTA2023_DR2data} with low-frequency observations from LOFAR. It
was obtained by refitting the EPTA timing model to the combined data with
software based on \textsc{tempo2}~\cite{Hobbs2006, Edwards2006}. The difference between
the observed and the predicted TOAs defines the timing residuals,
\begin{equation}
    \delta t_i = t^{\rm obs}_{{\rm TOA},i} - t^{\rm model}_{{\rm TOA},i}.
    \label{eq:residuals}
\end{equation}
Any effect not included in the timing model, such as noise processes or
gravitational waves, leaves a signature in the residuals.
Since the TOAs are typically collected every few weeks over a time span $T$ of
one or two decades, PTAs are sensitive to signals with frequencies between about $1/T$,
which is $1.6$\,nHz for $T = 20$\,yr, and the Nyquist frequency
$1/(2\Delta t)$ set by the observing cadence $\Delta t$, which is about
$4\times10^{-7}$\,Hz for observations every two weeks~\cite{Shaifullah2025}.
As an example, Figure~\ref{fig:residuals} shows the timing residuals of
PSR~J1640+2224, the pulsar analyzed in this work, in the DR2low dataset.
The residuals at low radio frequencies have a much
larger scatter, partly because of the larger TOA uncertainties and partly
because the effects of the ISM are stronger at low frequencies, as discussed in
Chapter~\ref{chpt:rumore}.

\section{Gravitational waves in pulsar timing} \label{sec:timingGW}

A gravitational wave passing between the Earth and a pulsar perturbs the
spacetime metric along the path of the radio pulses, slightly changing their
travel time. As a result, the rate at which the pulses are received is shifted
with respect to the intrinsic rotational frequency of the pulsar. This effect is
described by the redshift~\cite{Detweiler1979, Maggiore2018, Taylor2021}
\begin{equation}
    z(t) \equiv \frac{\nu_0 - \nu(t)}{\nu_0},
    \label{eq:redshift}
\end{equation}
where $\nu_0$ is the unperturbed pulse frequency and $\nu(t)$ the observed one.
Since the TOAs accumulate this shift over time, the contribution of the
gravitational wave to the timing residuals is given by its time integral,
\begin{equation}
    R(t) = \int_0^t z(t')\,dt'.
    \label{eq:R_GW}
\end{equation}
For a stochastic background, $R(t)$ is itself a random process and cannot be
predicted as a function of time. It is instead characterized by its power
spectral density, which follows from the strain spectral density $S_h(f)$
introduced in Section~\ref{sec:GWB}. Two factors relate the two quantities.
Averaging the response of the Earth--pulsar system over all propagation
directions of the background gives a factor $1/3$, while the time integral in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:R_GW} introduces a factor $1/(2\pi f)^2$. Using
Eq.~\eqref{eq:hc}, the PSD of the timing residuals induced by the GWB is~\cite{Taylor2021}
\begin{equation}
    S(f) = \frac{h_c^2(f)}{12\pi^2 f^3}.
    \label{eq:PSD_hc}
\end{equation}
Inserting the power-law spectrum of Eq.~\eqref{eq:hc_powerlaw}, expected for a
population of circular SMBHBs whose evolution is driven by GW emission, we obtain
\begin{equation}
    S(f) = \frac{\Agwb^2}{12\pi^2}\left(\frac{f}{\fyr}\right)^{-\gammagwb}\fyr^{-3},
    \qquad \gammagwb = \frac{13}{3},
    \label{eq:PSD_GWB}
\end{equation}
since $h_c^2/f^3 \propto f^{-4/3}\,f^{-3} = f^{-13/3}$.
This PSD is a power law with the same functional form used to describe the
intrinsic red noise of a pulsar. As a consequence, in the timing residuals of a
single pulsar the GWB is indistinguishable from intrinsic red noise. The signal
can only be identified by exploiting the fact that it is common to all the
pulsars of the array and induces a specific correlation between their residuals.

The correlation between the timing residuals of two pulsars $i$ and $j$ is
described by the Hellings--Downs curve~\cite{HellingsDowns1983}. Under the assumptions introduced in
Section~\ref{sec:GWB}, this correlation does not depend on the positions of the
pulsars on the sky, but only on their angular separation $\zeta_{ij}$,
\begin{equation}
    \Gamma_{ij} = \frac{3}{2}\,x_{ij}\ln x_{ij} - \frac{x_{ij}}{4} + \frac{1}{2}
    + \frac{1}{2}\,\delta_{ij},
    \qquad x_{ij} = \frac{1-\cos\zeta_{ij}}{2},
    \label{eq:HD}
\end{equation}
where $\delta_{ij}$ is the Kronecker delta.
The cross-PSD of the residuals of pulsars $i$ and $j$ is then
$S_{ij}(f) = \Gamma_{ij}\,S(f)$, with $S(f)$ given by Eq.~\eqref{eq:PSD_hc},
so that for a single pulsar ($\Gamma_{ii} = 1$) Eq.~\eqref{eq:PSD_hc} is recovered.

The Hellings--Downs curve is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:HD}. The correlation tends
to $1/2$ for pulsars that are close on the sky, reaches a minimum of about
$-0.15$ at $\zeta \simeq 82^\circ$, and rises again to $1/4$ for pulsars in
opposite directions. This angular pattern reflects the quadrupolar nature of
gravitational waves and is the distinctive signature of a GWB.

In 2023 four PTA collaborations, EPTA with InPTA, NANOGrav, PPTA and CPTA,
reported evidence for a common red process with Hellings--Downs correlations~\cite{EPTA2023_GWB, NANOGrav2023, PPTA2023, CPTA2023}. Since the signal is weak
and can be mimicked by the red noise of each pulsar, an accurate description of
the pulsar noise is essential. The noise sources and their
modeling are described in Chapter~\ref{chpt:rumore}.
