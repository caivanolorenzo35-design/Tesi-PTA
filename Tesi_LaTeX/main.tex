\chapter*{Abstract}

Millisecond pulsars (MSPs) are rapidly rotating neutron stars that emit
radio pulses with a regularity comparable to that of atomic clocks. A
gravitational wave crossing the space between the Earth and a pulsar
perturbs the travel time of the pulses, which arrive slightly earlier or
later than predicted. By monitoring for decades the times of arrival
(TOAs) of the pulses from a set of pulsars, a pulsar timing array (PTA),
it is therefore possible to observe gravitational waves with frequencies
of the order of nanohertz.

In this band the main expected source is the stochastic gravitational wave
background (GWB), produced by the incoherent superposition of the signals
emitted by the population of supermassive black hole binaries. The GWB
appears as a low-frequency signal common to all pulsars and correlated
among them in a characteristic way. In 2023 several collaborations,
including the European Pulsar Timing Array (EPTA), reported first evidence
for it.

The detection of the GWB is made difficult by the noise sources that
affect the TOAs. These are the irregularities in the rotation of the
pulsar (intrinsic red noise), the variations of the electron density along
the line of sight (dispersion measure, DM, variations) and the variations
of the scattering of the pulses in the interstellar medium. These
processes produce a red noise in the timing residuals, with more power at
low frequencies, described by the same power-law spectrum as the GWB. The
intrinsic red noise is achromatic, while the DM and scattering variations
are chromatic, since their amplitude depends on the radio frequency of the
observation. In a single pulsar the GWB is therefore hard to distinguish
from the noise, and the components of the noise model can be degenerate
with one another. An accurate noise model is thus essential for the search
for the GWB.

In this thesis we study the degeneracies among the parameters of the noise
model of a single pulsar, PSR~J1640+2224, in the DR2low dataset, which
combines the second data release of the EPTA with low-frequency
observations of LOFAR. The noise model has six free parameters, an
amplitude and a spectral index for each of the three processes. The
analysis is performed with \textsc{Discovery}, a recent code based on JAX,
and we first verify that it gives the same posterior distributions as
\textsc{Enterprise}, the reference code of the community. The comparison
with the dataset without the LOFAR observations shows that the
low-frequency data are necessary to constrain the chromatic noise. With
DR2low the posterior of the DM parameters has two peaks, and the contours
between the DM and the scattering parameters are L-shaped, the sign of a
degeneracy between the two chromatic processes. This structure does not
disappear if the chromatic index of the scattering variations is left
free, or if a band noise, a red process that acts only on the TOAs of the
LOFAR band, is added to the model. The band noise is not detected, and
only an upper limit on its amplitude is obtained.

To understand the origin of this structure we analyze simulated datasets
with the same characteristics as the real data. When the simulated data
contain only the three processes of the model, the injected parameters are
recovered and the degeneracy does not appear, so the frequency coverage of
DR2low is sufficient to distinguish the two chromatic processes. We then
inject processes that are not included in the model. A GWB with the
amplitudes measured by the PTA collaborations produces only weak L-shaped
tails in the contours between the DM and the scattering parameters, while
a stronger GWB is absorbed by the intrinsic red noise. A band noise biases
the DM parameters toward higher amplitudes and lower spectral indices, in
the region of the high-amplitude peak of the real data. Because of the
large uncertainties of the LOFAR TOAs, this band noise is not detected
when it is added to the model, and the upper limit obtained from the real
data is similar to the one obtained from the simulation. The presence of
band noise in the real data therefore cannot be excluded.

None of the simulations reproduces the whole structure observed in the
real data, whose origin remains open. These results show that a process
that is present in the data but not in the model can bias the parameters
of the other noise components, and that an accurate noise model for each
pulsar is an essential step in the search for the GWB.
